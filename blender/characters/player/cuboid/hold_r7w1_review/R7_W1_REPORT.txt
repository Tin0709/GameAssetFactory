2. LONG GUN HOLD STUDY

The rifle is higher, more level, and closer at the stock. Its grip rises
171 mm; the stock-butt center rises 106 mm and moves 51 mm toward the body
relative to A. The gun is shifted toward the character's right shoulder to
avoid the lower head. This improves the shoulder-braced intent, but is an
artistic compromise rather than a finished, anatomically natural hold.

The trigger arm is pulled back and directed toward the receiver. A 45 mm
pose translation draws that shoulder back relative to A; bone rest data and
length are unchanged. The support arm crosses forward with a different
orientation, but its block endpoint gathers near the rear receiver rather
than achieving the reference's convincing bent fore-end support.

Spine/chest rotation creates mild yaw and forward participation. The chest
uses 3 degrees forward rotation and -7 degrees local axial rotation, versus
2 and -4 in A. Head and neck incline toward the weapon. The torso and head
help the higher hold, although the straight arm beams remain conspicuous.

4. VISUAL COMPARISON

Views: elevated gameplay three-quarter, front three-quarter, side, rear
three-quarter, native gameplay distance, and close weapon inspection.
Use the individual matched comparison sheets for fair camera/scale/lighting
comparison. The A/B/C shared-scene video is convenient for timing review;
its spatially separated characters receive slightly different illumination.
The native gameplay-distance sheet preserves the original 1200x720 frames.

1) Stock-to-shoulder: A reads lower and more forward. B/C raise the stock into
   the right shoulder region, closer to the reference intent. Stock-center
   distance to an explicitly chosen upper-chest landmark falls from 201 mm
   to 72 mm. This is a landmark metric, NOT a measured air gap or proof of
   physical stock contact. The outboard shoulder placement still needs a
   human judgement of how firmly it feels braced.
2) Trigger-arm silhouette: B/C separate near-body control from the support
   role, but the arm remains a long straight block. The reference's compact
   returning/bent silhouette cannot be formed with this single rigid segment.
3) Support-arm silhouette: B/C point farther across and forward, but the hand
   does not reach the intended far support helper. From the elevated view,
   the two block hands still bunch together. This is the weakest part of the
   study versus the references and is not a successful reference match.
4) Rifle height/depth: B/C have a stronger, nearly level aiming line. The grip
   is 171 mm higher and 16.5 mm closer along forward depth; the stock is 51 mm
   closer. Outboard placement buys head clearance but exposes arm reach costs.
5) Torso yaw/lean: B/C have mild spine/chest asymmetry and receive the gun more
   clearly than A. They retain the existing blocky body proportions and legs.
6) Head/gaze: B brings the head into a more weapon-aware relationship. C adds
   the 3.20-degree focus gesture. The eyes remain visible in the principal
   front/gameplay views. This is an impression of focus, not calibrated eye
   alignment through a particular sight aperture.
7) Clipping/overlap: final sampled B/C have no weapon-triangle intersection
   with the inset head/chest boxes. Trigger hand/weapon penetration remains;
   the block grip and crowded hands are visibly coarse. See section 5.
8) Gameplay readability: the higher rifle silhouette distinguishes B/C from
   A at small size, but hand roles become less clear and C differs only subtly
   from B. The supplied stills use closer, dramatic framing; that appeal does
   not by itself prove gameplay readability. The new hold improves the armed
   silhouette, while failing to reproduce the references' arm structure.
9) Rig limitation evidence: each arm has one 675 mm rigid bone and an eight-
   vertex block, with no elbow/forearm articulation. In B the far support
   helper is 937 mm from the left shoulder. Its gap to the terminal hand box
   is 261 mm (A: 200 mm). These numbers describe that specific helper, not a
   universal gap from every weapon surface. The right grip helper is 18 mm
   outside the terminal hand box toward the wrist. Bringing the stock up
   improves bracing but forces visible straight beams and compromised hands.

5. CLIPPING / LIMITATIONS

The contact probe tests weapon triangles against bone-bound boxes inset by
5 mm. Final B and sampled C frames 1,7,15,20,23,31,43,49 report zero head/chest
intersections. This is a sampled weapon/body test, not exhaustive collision
validation for every body pair, subframe, or view.

Some weapon insertion into a fingerless block hand is acceptable for this
bounded study. However, the right block has 110 intersecting weapon triangles
and a maximum tested vertex depth of about 70 mm (A: about 79 mm). That is
not a claim of universally minor overlap: the deep block contact and grip
near the wrist are unresolved and should not be approved as production polish.
The support hand/fore-end relationship and crowded-hand silhouette are also
unacceptable for the requested reference quality. The facial penetration
found in an earlier, higher candidate was resolved by the final lower/outboard
placement; blocking/high_stock images are earlier evidence, not final poses.

Both new Actions have exact endpoint matrix agreement, no keyed scale,
and unchanged root/hips/leg pose bases over quarter-frame sampling. Those
checks protect the setup; they do not remove the artistic arm limitations.

6. RECOMMENDATION

CURRENT RIG IS INSUFFICIENT; NEXT STEP SHOULD BE A RIG UPGRADE FOR ARMS.

Keep these Actions as evidence and an upper-body direction study. Do not
promote this hold to production. A separately approved arm articulation pass
should enable a compact trigger elbow and independently directed forearm/
support hand. An elbow alone does not solve the 937 mm far-helper reach with
a 675 mm total arm: the support contact zone and gun placement must also be
chosen together, preserving intended character proportions.
No arm upgrade or production integration was performed in R7-W1.
